THE FIELD GUIDE

Every lesson in the game — and the one behind them all. No dying required.

The question

Every founder eventually meets one governing question, usually without noticing: of all the decisions this company makes, which ones are mine? Answer it too greedily and you become the company's rate-limiting step. Answer it too generously and the company quietly loses its keel. The game lets you die both ways in an afternoon — this page is the map of what you died of.

The two cartoon deaths

Fast, loud, and rare in the wild — but they make the mechanisms visible.

OPTION A The Bottleneck — “every decision is my job”

  • First order, you win: your decisions really are better. Second order, everyone else's decisions stop existing — and a company is mostly everyone else.
  • A queue compounds like debt. Every late decision delays ten downstream ones, and the interest is paid in missed markets.
  • People who love deciding leave; people who stay learn helplessness. You didn't hire a passive org — you selected for one.
  • Overload makes you worse precisely when it matters: the bottleneck's quality collapses exactly at peak load. You become the rate-limiting enzyme of the whole organism.
  • You don't end up making the best decisions. You end up making all of them — briefly the same thing, then the opposite.

OPTION B The Ghost — “you guys figure it out”

  • First order, you win: real speed, real morale, real shipping. Second order, incoherence compounds silently — four local optimums, zero global one.
  • One-way doors get walked through casually: pricing, data, decade-long ink. Reversible mistakes wash out; irreversible ones accumulate like sediment, then like concrete.
  • Principal–agent, live on stage: every lieutenant optimizes their kingdom honestly and locally. Nobody owns the whole, so the whole belongs to no one.
  • Without an arbiter, disagreements don't resolve — they metastasize into politics, and politics eats the calendar that used to build things.
  • Empower everyone to steer and the ship gets eight rudders and no keel.

The two common traps

Slow, quiet, and where most founders actually live — because each one comes with evidence that you're doing it right, and neither produces the feedback that would correct it. Nothing burns. That's the mechanism.

OPTION A½ The Final Say — “I delegate everything! …except what matters”

  • Count leverage, not decisions. Delegating 94% by number and 6% by weight is hoarding with better paperwork.
  • Every “critical” is a claim on your bandwidth. If the critical list never shrinks as your people grow, the list is measuring your anxiety, not the stakes.
  • A review that adds two weeks and 4% is negative-sum. Some calls need your standard; most need your speed — or your absence.
  • The insidious failures give no feedback: no fires, no exits, no crisis. Just a pond. If everything is calm and nothing compounds, that is the alarm.
  • The heavy calls cost emotional energy — which is exactly why you keep them, and exactly why releasing a few buys the most leverage per unit of trust.

OPTION B½ The Conductor — “the big calls are how people grow”

  • Delegation is a bet, and the odds are set by context, incentive, and experience — not by how much you believe in someone. Believe in people; bet like an actuary.
  • A critical decision is not a training ground. Grow people on reversible calls with real stakes, not on the ones that set the company's ceiling.
  • Consensus averages; markets don't. The midpoint of three opinions is usually nobody's right answer.
  • The hard calls — killing the beloved thing, moving on the beloved person — can only be made centrally. If they're “everyone's,” they're no one's, and they appreciate with interest.
  • Beware metrics that only measure comfort. A 4.9 culture score with flat growth means the feedback loop has been sedated, not that the org is healthy.

The narrow path

What Option C is drilling into your hands.

  • Sort every decision with two questions: how bad if wrong? and can we undo it? Irreversible-and-important comes to you. Everything else goes to whoever is closest to the facts.
  • Beware the costumes: the “tidy-up” that breaks three hundred partners is a one-way door; the enormous strategic partnership with a 30-day exit clause is a revolving one. Stakes and reversibility live in the details, not the title.
  • Delegate outcomes, not tasks — and review after, not before. Feedback compounds into judgment; approval compounds into a queue.
  • The middle move exists: delegate it, memo first, review after. On heavy calls it grows your org at a discount; on trivial ones it's just process tax. Match the ceremony to the weight.
  • Write your principles down until people can predict your call. The endgame is decisions arriving at your desk already sorted — then you take a vacation and nothing breaks.

The meta-lesson

There is no right place on the dial. That is the lesson under all the other lessons, and it's the one worth paying for: anyone who tells you the correct percentage of decisions a CEO should make is selling a framework, and the framework will be wrong by Christmas. The correct setting moves — down as your people compound, up hard the week the batteries catch fire, down again the week the recall ends, down for good as your principles start making your calls without you in the room. A fixed philosophy, including a wise one, is just a slower way to drift.

Drift is the real enemy, and drift is silent — that's what makes it dangerous. Both failure directions feel like virtue from the inside: gripping feels like diligence, releasing feels like leadership. Each pays its first-order dividend immediately — better calls, faster shipping — and mails the second-order bill quarters later, addressed to someone you used to be. You cannot feel drift. By the time you can feel it, it's a reorg. You can only instrument for it.

So the skill — the actual, learnable, compounding skill of governing a company — is not finding the balance point. It is: build the gauges, glance at them weekly, and correct early and small. Your queue is the grip gauge: if it grows, your bar for “critical” is too low. Your surprise rate is the trust gauge: if your own company keeps startling you, the bar is too high. Recount the “critical” list every quarter — it should shrink as people grow, spike in crisis, and never, ever be static. You will always be a little wrong; that's the terrain, not a failure. The CEOs who compound are simply wrong in a direction they're watching, briefly, with a hand already on the dial.

The instruments

Drift can't be felt, only measured. Two gauge clusters, one glance a week:

GRIP GAUGES — “am I the bottleneck?”TRUST GAUGES — “have I let go of the keel?”
Your decision queue: length, and the age of the oldest item. Your surprise rate: how often the company does something you'd have vetoed.
Latency: how long a team waits between asking and shipping. Duplicate work discovered at demos; incompatible roadmaps, all funded.
Proposals written in your voice — approval-seeking dressed as thinking. Decisions landing on the midpoint of three opinions.
Your calendar: how many meetings exist to extract a yes from you. The hard call everyone knows about that nobody has made for two quarters.
Needle moving? Your “critical” bar is too low. Loosen five points. Needle moving? Your “critical” bar is too high. Tighten five points.

The decision memo (print this page — this box is the takeaway)

SUNBEAM-STYLE DECISION MEMO — ADAPT AND POST WHERE EVERYONE CAN SEE IT

Comes to me (aim for a single-digit count per quarter — recount quarterly):

  1. One-way doors: choices we can't cheaply undo — pricing structure, platform bets, data policy, decade-long ink, anything personally guaranteed.
  2. Bet-the-company allocations: the second product, the acquisition, the round.
  3. Culture-setting hires and exits — the people who will hire the next forty.
  4. Cross-team ties nobody else can break (I break them fast; slow ties are my failure).
  5. Whatever this quarter's crisis centralizes — with a written trigger for giving it back.

Never comes to me (everything else, and out loud):

  1. Anything reversible for less than it costs to ask me. Decide it near the facts; tell me after, if it's interesting.
  2. The middle weight: decide it, but write the two-pager first — recommendation plus one honest dissent — and give me 24 hours to read it. I'll rarely use them.
  3. My review happens after shipping, in writing, on a weekly rhythm. Feedback, not approval.

My gauges, checked weekly: queue length · oldest item · surprises this week · duplicates found

This quarter's reflection (three lines, honestly): Which “critical” item was actually reversible?
What surprised me that shouldn't have?
Which decision should someone else make next quarter?

Further reading

  • Jeff Bezos, Amazon shareholder letters (1997 & 2015) — the origin of Type 1 / Type 2 decisions: one-way doors deserve process; two-way doors deserve speed and forgiveness.
  • Andy Grove, High Output Management — decisions belong at the lowest level with the competence to make them; the manager's job is to raise that level.
  • Jensen & Meckling, “Theory of the Firm” (1976) — the principal–agent problem in its natural habitat: why honest, capable people optimizing locally still drift the whole.
  • Sull & Eisenhardt, Simple Rules — how a few written principles let many people make one person's decisions, faster and eventually better.
